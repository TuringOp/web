Dependently Typed World Models

A world model is, at heart, a machine for rehearsing reality. It takes the rush and clatter of perception, compresses that stream into a latent account of what matters, and then asks a delicate question: if I do this next, what sort of world will I wake up in? The ambition is grand, almost novelistic. The danger is that the model's confidence often outruns its discipline. Dependent type theory offers a way to turn that confidence into something sterner: an imagination that carries its obligations with it.

World models

The broad strokes: a history of machine imagination

The phrase world model has worn many costumes. In classical control, it meant a state-space model: equations that advanced a system from one instant to the next. In cognitive science, it suggested an internal picture of the world, the sort of thing an animal might carry while deciding whether a rustle in the grass is wind or predator. In modern machine learning, especially in model-based reinforcement learning, it has come to mean a learned simulator: a neural structure that predicts observations, rewards, or latent dynamics well enough that an agent can plan with it.

The attraction is obvious. A system that can imagine futures before it spends a real action in the environment can be more sample-efficient, more strategic, and more explainable than one that learns only by reflex. A world model gives planning a stage. It lets an agent try on possible futures, discard clumsy ones, and move through the real world with the practiced air of someone who has already been there in rehearsal.

Yet world models have always carried two intertwined promises. The first is instrumental: they can improve performance. The second is interpretive: they seem to offer a glimpse into what the system thinks the world is. That second promise is part of their glamour. If a model can reconstruct the scene ahead, forecast the reward curve, and evolve an internal state over time, then it feels as if we are no longer dealing with a mere function approximator but with something closer to a theory of the environment.

A local specimen

What the repository's /world_models directory actually does

The local /world_models directory is a useful, concrete specimen. It is not a metaphorical world model. It is an engineering library for visual planning. The README describes it as a platform-agnostic toolkit for agents that plan from images, and the surrounding structure makes the scope legible: agents/ holds model families such as PlaNet, SV2P, and pure reward variants; planners/ contains the decision procedures that query those models; simulate/ executes full episodes; tasks/ wraps environments from DeepMind Control and Atari; and configs/ records experiment configurations for different planning regimes.

Several ideas in the code are especially revealing. In world_models/planners/planners.py, the CEM planner is defined as a planning loop over learned dynamics. It observes the previous image, action, and reward; tiles them across many candidate futures; generates action proposals; asks the model to predict consequences; scores those predictions; and then refits its sampling distribution around the best trajectories. This is not reactive control. It is deliberation under a learned surrogate of the world.

In world_models/simulate/simulate.py, the library shows its operational purpose with unusual clarity. A single episode is built from a running ledger of observations, actions, rewards, and the model's predictions. The code computes approximate values, stacks per-step records into episode arrays, and returns both what happened and what the model believed would happen. That pairing is the moral center of the library: reality in one hand, prediction in the other.

The README adds an important empirical correction to the popular story. Reward prediction alone, it says, is usually not enough in online visual model-based reinforcement learning; image prediction matters more, and image reconstruction error correlates more strongly with eventual performance. That is an instructive finding. It suggests that a useful world model is not merely a reward oracle. It must preserve enough of the world's texture to support exploration, not just enough of its payoff surface to imitate good luck.

Explanatory power

What world models explain, and what they often leave implicit

For all their appeal, ordinary world models usually explain in the wrong register. They can tell us that certain futures were scored highly, that certain frames were reconstructed sharply, that certain latents are stable under rollout. But they are less good at telling us what must remain true no matter how the latent state evolves. They do not naturally distinguish between a state that is merely likely and a state that is admissible. They do not insist, at the type level, that a plan preserve provenance, resource ownership, privacy budgets, or contractual boundaries.

This is where many contemporary systems become eloquent and vague at the same time. The model can say, in effect, “I predict a future like this.” What it cannot easily say is, “Only futures of this certified shape are even representable to me.” In most ML stacks, such rules live in comments, validators, or post-hoc monitoring. They are external to the model's ontology.

The gap is subtle but consequential: predictive competence is not the same thing as constructive legitimacy. A system can forecast outcomes accurately while still lacking a typed account of which outcomes are structurally permissible.

Dependent type theory

How dependent types change the conversation

Dependent type theory begins from a sternly practical idea: the shape of data can depend on the value of data, and proofs can travel with programs as first-class companions. Instead of storing a latent state and hoping later checks will certify it, one can store a latent state together with evidence that it satisfies an invariant. Instead of defining a transition function over all nominal states, one can define transitions only over states that are already known to be well-formed.

In a theorem prover such as the Rocq Prover, this changes software from something that is merely executable into something that can be constructive in a literal sense. An object is not just a value; it can be a value plus the evidence required to inhabit a specification. A planner is not merely a function from history to action; it can be a function from well-typed histories to actions whose safety obligations are discharged as part of construction.

The conceptual gain for world models is immediate. Latent state no longer needs to be a bag of tensors floating in trust. It can be recast as a proof-carrying state: observations with lineage, rewards with admissibility conditions, action proposals with budget witnesses, and rollouts whose compositional steps preserve formally stated invariants.

The Rocq bridge

Why /fcsl-pcm matters: partial commutative monoids as a semantics of resource

The local /fcsl-pcm directory gives this vision a disciplined substrate. Its README describes the project as a formalization of partial commutative monoids, or PCMs, for use in separation logic and the verification of pointer-manipulating sequential and concurrent programs. If that sounds remote from machine learning, it is only because most ML systems have not yet learned to describe their own resources with this degree of care.

In pcm/pcm.v, the library defines the core algebra: a notion of validity, a partial join, a unit element, and proofs that the join is commutative and associative where defined. This is a language for talking about pieces of state that can be combined only when they are compatible. In pcm/heap.v, heaps are modeled as disjoint fragments of memory. In pcm/natmap.v, histories and maps over non-zero naturals become compositional structures that support reasoning about traces, ownership, and ordered records.

That is precisely the sort of mathematics a serious world model lacks. A learned simulator usually knows how to compose predictions numerically. It rarely knows how to compose responsibilities. PCMs offer a way to make resource boundaries first-class: which observations are owned by which subsystem, which trace fragments may be combined, which cached beliefs are mutually compatible, and when a planner is attempting to join states that should remain separate.

Illustrative Rocq sketches

Small constructive examples from the ground up

The snippets below are intentionally small and illustrative. They are written in Rocq style, borrowing the vocabulary of /fcsl-pcm to show how a world model can be described as a proof-carrying object rather than a free-floating predictor.

Sample 1 · A proof-carrying world interface 
From mathcomp Require Import ssreflect ssrbool ssrnat seq.
From pcm Require Import pcm.

Section TypedWorld.
Context {State : pcm}.

Record WorldModel := {
  observe : State -> seq nat -> State;
  imagine : State -> seq nat -> State;
  admissible : State -> Prop;
  observe_ok : forall s frame,
    admissible s -> admissible (observe s frame);
  imagine_ok : forall s actions,
    admissible s -> valid (imagine s actions)
}.

End TypedWorld.

The important move is not syntactic but philosophical. A world model is defined together with its preservation lemmas. One does not bolt safety on after the fact; one demands it at the interface.

Sample 2 · A typed rollout trace using histories from natmap 
From mathcomp Require Import ssreflect ssrbool ssrnat seq.
From pcm Require Import pcm natmap.

Record rollout := {
  trace : history nat;
  horizon : nat;
  horizon_pos : horizon > 0
}.

Definition extend_trace (r : rollout) (t reward : nat) :=
  if t == 0 then None
  else if valid ((t \-> reward) \+ trace r)
       then Some {| trace := (t \-> reward) \+ trace r;
                    horizon := horizon r;
                    horizon_pos := horizon_pos r |}
       else None.

Here a rollout is not just a sequence. It carries a positive horizon witness, and each extension succeeds only when the history fragment can be validly joined with what is already known. In other words, the trace grows only through compatible evidence.

Sample 3 · Heap-style ownership for a sensor boundary 
From mathcomp Require Import ssreflect ssrbool.
From pcm Require Import heap.

Definition sensor_write (p : ptr) (v : dynamic id) (h : heap) :=
  if p == null then Undef else upd p v h.

Lemma sensor_write_preserves_nonnull p v h :
  p != null ->
  sensor_write p v h != Undef.
Proof.
move=> p_nonnull.
rewrite /sensor_write p_nonnull.
case: h=> //= fm pf.
by case: decP=> // /eqP.
Qed.

The exact engineering boundary in a deployed ML system would be richer than this toy example, but the pattern is already visible: sensing, caching, and actuation can be represented as resource transitions with explicit side conditions. A world model that issues an action could be required to prove that it owns the facts on which that action depends.

Putting the ideas together

How dependent type theory could give world models new purpose

The marriage of these two directories—/world_models for learned predictive structure and /fcsl-pcm for proof-oriented resource composition—suggests a world model with a more serious civic life. Not merely a predictor of pixels. Not merely a scorer of rewards. Rather, a system that can say: this latent state is assembled from legitimate fragments; this imagined trajectory respects ownership and budget constraints; this action depends only on observations whose provenance is still valid; this explanation is not a retrospective story but a checked witness.

Such a model would be especially powerful in settings where the relevant environment is partly social or institutional: multi-agent operations, data pipelines, policy-constrained automation, secure tool use, or financial execution. In these domains, the world is not just physics. It is a fabric of permissions, scopes, obligations, and revocations. Dependent typing is well suited to that fabric because it can express states whose legality depends on values and histories, not just on nominal schemas.

Security implications

Why this matters for the security of AI and ML systems

Typed provenance

Observations, embeddings, tool outputs, and retrieved artifacts can carry machine-checked provenance constraints. A planner can then be forbidden, by construction, from acting on data whose origin or scope is uncertified.

Compositional isolation

PCM-style joins make incompatible resources explicit. That offers a formal language for separating secrets, tenants, model contexts, and temporary capabilities before they become a silent blob inside a latent state.

Proof-carrying actions

High-risk actions—publishing, exfiltrating, transferring, escalating privileges, mutating production state—can be gated on witnesses that the relevant preconditions truly hold.

More legible failures

When a proof obligation cannot be discharged, the system has a crisp reason to stop. That turns certain classes of security failure from murky postmortems into immediate type errors or failed joins.

The practical result is not invulnerability. It is a different failure surface. Instead of hoping that monitoring will notice dangerous behavior after a model has improvised it, one can pre-structure the space of representable and executable behaviors. In security work, that shift—from detection to construction—is often the whole game.

Closing

A stricter imagination

World models began as a way to let machines picture the road ahead. Dependent type theory offers a way to insist that the road, the traveler, and the permission to travel are all described in the same language. The combination is appealing not because it makes AI systems less ambitious, but because it makes their ambition answerable.

A dependently typed world model would not prevent every operational failure, nor abolish the need for policy, monitoring, and human judgment. But it could force more of an AI system's assumptions into machine-checked form. And in that sense it is plausible that some high-profile exfiltration-style episodes in the broader ecosystem—including incidents discussed around OpenAI such as the ExploitGym Hugging Face exfiltration—might have been harder to stage, easier to detect at the point of action, or impossible to justify within the typed boundary of the system itself.
